## The English language model

Generated by `tooling/build-language-model.ts` from 14 public-domain novels (~7.7 M letters):

| Table                                                  | Size    | Stored as                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------- | -------------------------------- |
| Letter unigram frequencies                             | 26      | probabilities                    |
| Conditional bigrams P(next \| prev), add-½ smoothed    | 26 × 26 | centi-bels `round(−100·log10 p)` |
| Word-initial / word-final letter distributions         | 2 × 26  | centi-bels                       |
| Most frequent quadgrams                                | 3 000   | centi-bels                       |
| Vocabulary, frequency-ranked (+ modern/internet words) | ~6 000  | space-separated string           |

Integer centi-bels keep the generated file compact and diff-friendly. Quadgrams outside the top 3 000
**back off** to a first-order Markov estimate (unigram × three bigram transitions, discounted by
0.35 bels) instead of a flat floor, which sharply improves the separation between English and
gibberish for short texts. Word probabilities follow Zipf's law: `p(rank) ≈ 0.08 / rank`.

## Leet encoding

1. **Word level** (optional): slang substitution (`the → teh`, `owned → pwn3d`) and morphology
   rules (`-cks → x`, `-er → xor`, plural `-s → z`), preserving capitalisation patterns.
2. **Character level**: each letter is substituted with probability `density`, choosing from the
   active glyph list by strategy — _preferred_ (first glyph), _random_ (seeded) or _cycle_
   (round-robin per letter, maximising variety).
3. **Case styling** for the letters that remain: preserve, lower, upper, alternating or random.

Glyph lists come from a tiered database (readable → classic → hardcore ASCII art → Cyrillic
look-alikes); presets are orderings over tiers, and the Custom preset is a user-edited map.

**Determinism.** Within every built-in preset the glyph sets of different letters are disjoint and
no glyph is a plain letter, so encoding is injective at the glyph level: no two letters can produce
the same symbol, whatever the strategy. That is why the default (classic) preset maps _i_ → `1` and
leaves _l_ unchanged — `1` and `|` are popular for both letters in the wild, and a preset that used
them for both would make `1` ambiguous. Such folk glyphs (`1` and `|` for l, `ph` for f, `vv` for w,
`z` for s, `13` for b, `12` for r, `9` for q…) live in a separate decode-only table: the encoder never
emits them, the decoder still reads `1337` and `ph34r`. A unit test enforces the property for every
preset.

## Scytale

The rod carries `d` letters per turn (its thickness). An `n`-letter message is laid out in a grid of
`d` rows and `⌈n / d⌉` columns, written row by row (along the rod) and read column by column (one turn
of the strip after another). When `n` is not a multiple of `d` the last row is short; the empty cells
are blank strip, not padding, and are skipped in both directions, so encoding and decoding are exact
inverse permutations for every length. With `d = 4`, `Iamhurtverybadlyhelp` becomes
`Iryyatbhmvaehedlurlp`, the standard textbook example.

## Probabilistic leet decoding

Decoding is ambiguous (`1` = i/l, `|` = i/l/part of `|-|`, `ph` = f or "ph"). Eleet treats it as a
search for the most probable segmentation of each whitespace-delimited chunk:

- **Candidates** at every position: every glyph (all tiers, longest first) that matches there, the
  literal letter, or the literal character.
- **Score** of a hypothesis = Σ bigram log-probabilities (word-initial distribution at word starts)
  - glyph priors (per tier; glyphs made of ASCII letters are penalised because they compete with a
    literal reading) + a penalty for keeping a glyph character literally
  - at each word end: the word-final distribution and a **dictionary bonus** for vocabulary words
    (including simple inflections such as `-s`, `-ed`, `-ing`).
- **Beam search** (width 24), deduplicated on the current word buffer, keeps it linear in the input.
- **Post-processing** reverses slang (`teh → the`, `haxxor → hacker`) and restores SHOUTED case.
- Pure numbers are kept unless they spell one of the 400 most frequent words (`15 → is`, `1984`
  stays), which prevents the classic "born in lqb4" failure.

On a mixed benchmark paragraph the decoder recovers 98–100 % of words across all presets and random
strategies in about 10 ms.

## Language scoring

`languageScore(text) ∈ [0, 1]` combines printability², the share of letters, spacing, normalised
quadgram fitness (mapped from [−7.5, −4] to [0, 1]) and vocabulary coverage. Persian text takes a
separate branch (Arabic-script share and common Persian words), so Magic recognises decoded Farsi.

## Magic: multi-layer auto-decoding

A beam search over ~30 decoder "recipes" (Base64/32/58/85, hex/binary/octal/decimal, URL, HTML,
Unicode escapes, Punycode, Morse in two alphabets, NATO, Braille, A1Z26, Bacon, ROT13/47, Atbash,
reverse, upside-down, fancy-text normalisation, Zalgo stripping, zero-width reveal, homoglyph
skeletons, leet decoding and the English→Persian keyboard fix):

- each recipe has a cheap `applies()` guard (usually the tool's `detect()` regex), pruning most of
  the tree;
- Caesar contributes a single branch — the best shift by χ² — instead of 25;
- every output is scored with `languageScore`, deduplicated, and the best `beam` nodes of each depth
  are expanded further (default depth 3, beam 6);
- final ranking subtracts 0.015 per extra step, favouring simpler explanations.

## The cipher cracker

| Cipher              | Attack                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Caesar              | all 25 shifts                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Affine              | all 312 keys (12 multipliers coprime with 26 × 26 offsets)                                                                                                                                         |
| Atbash              | single candidate                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| Vigenère / Beaufort | periods ranked by mean column index of coincidence (Friedman); for the best four periods each column is solved as a shift cipher by minimum χ²; repeated keys are collapsed (`LEMONLEMON → LEMON`) |
| Rail fence          | all rail counts 2–12 and offsets (canonical offset preferred on near-ties)                                                                                                                         |
| Columnar            | every permutation for 2–7 columns (Heap's algorithm), a keyword reconstructed from the winner                                                                                                      |

Candidates are ranked by quadgram fitness; the report keeps the top three plus any candidate within
60 % of the winner's confidence.

## Frequency analysis and cipher fingerprinting

The verdict uses the index of coincidence (≈ 0.0667 for English, ≈ 0.0385 for random letters),
χ² against English letter frequencies (normalised by length) and quadgram fitness:

| IoC         | χ²/N | Fitness | Verdict                                    |
| ----------- | ---- | ------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| ≥ 0.056     | any  | high    | English plaintext                          |
| ≥ 0.056     | low  | low     | Transposition (right letters, wrong order) |
| ≥ 0.056     | high | low     | Monoalphabetic substitution                |
| 0.043–0.056 | —    | —       | Polyalphabetic (key-length estimate shown) |
| < 0.043     | —    | —       | Random / long-key / modern cipher          |

## Enigma

Rotors I–VIII with their historical wirings and notches, reflectors A, B and C, ring settings, start
positions and a plugboard. Stepping follows the pawl mechanism, including the **double step**: when
the middle rotor sits on its notch it advances together with the left rotor on the next key press.
Each rotor pass computes `(wiring[(c + p − r) mod 26] − p + r) mod 26`. Verified against the standard
`AAAAA → BDZGO` vector, the `ADU → ADV → AEW → BFX` stepping sequence and the worked example of the
1930 German army manual (UKW-A, rotors II I III, rings XMV, plugboard AM FI NV PS TU WZ, message key
ABL), which decrypts to `FEIND LIQEI NFANT ERIEK OLONN …`.

## Invisible ink

- **Zero-width scheme**: UTF-8 bytes → base-4 digits written with U+200B, U+200C, U+200D and U+2060,
  framed by U+FEFF markers. The framing is what makes decoding robust: legitimate ZWNJ in Persian
  text and ZWJ inside emoji sequences outside the frame are ignored.
- **Unicode Tags scheme ("ASCII smuggling")**: each ASCII character maps to its invisible twin in
  U+E0000–U+E007F, wrapped in LANGUAGE TAG … CANCEL TAG; non-ASCII characters are percent-encoded
  first.

## Homoglyphs and the Unicode Inspector

Encoding substitutes Latin letters with visually identical Cyrillic, Greek, Armenian and Cherokee
characters at a chosen density. Decoding maps every known confusable back to ASCII (a "skeleton", in
the spirit of Unicode Technical Standard #39). The Inspector flags invisible and bidi-control
characters (Trojan Source), tag characters, private-use code points, combining marks, unusual spaces
and — most tellingly — **words that mix Latin with look-alike scripts**. General category and script
come from the regex engine's `\p{…}` support, so no Unicode database ships with the app.

## AES sealed boxes

`Base64( "EL" ‖ version ‖ iterations(u32 BE) ‖ salt(16) ‖ nonce(12) ‖ ciphertext ‖ tag(16) )`

The key is derived with PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-256 (default 600 000 iterations) and used with AES-256-GCM.
The 7-byte header is passed as **additional authenticated data**, so tampering with the version or
iteration count fails authentication just like tampering with the ciphertext. Iteration counts
outside 1 000–10 000 000 are rejected before any work is done.

## Keccak / SHA-3 without BigInt

Keccak-f[1600] keeps each 64-bit lane as two 32-bit halves in `Uint32Array`s, implementing 64-bit
rotations with shifts across the halves. The sponge absorbs at the rate for the output size, with
domain padding `0x06` for SHA-3 (FIPS 202) or `0x01` for the original Keccak used by Ethereum. The
implementation is checked against published vectors and against Node's OpenSSL on random inputs
around the rate boundaries (135/136/137 bytes).

## Finglish transliteration

Persian script omits short vowels, so a letter-by-letter transliteration can only guess. Eleet layers
three strategies:

1. a **lexicon** of everyday words with their conventional Finglish spellings (and accepted variants
   for the reverse direction);
2. **context rules** for the letters that can be consonant or vowel (`و` = v/o, `ی` = y/i, final
   `ه` = e), silent `و` in `خوا`, and morpheme splitting at ZWNJ;
3. **phonotactics**: Persian syllables are CV(C)(C), so a word never starts with two consonants and
   never stacks three — the unwritten vowel is restored at exactly those points.

Finglish → Persian parses digraphs (`kh`, `sh`, `ch`, `gh`, `zh`), writes only long vowels (as Persian
orthography does), drops the unwritten ezafe (`doost e man`) and uses the lexicon when possible.

## T9 prediction and Pig Latin decoding

Both reuse the vocabulary: T9 maps every vocabulary word to its key sequence and picks the most
frequent match for each digit group; Pig Latin decoding enumerates every legal consonant-cluster split
(`qu` and leading `y` handled) and chooses the most frequent resulting word.
